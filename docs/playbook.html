Field guide · process-first AI deployment

The FDE Playbook

How a forward deployed engineer finds the real process inside a business, rebuilds it with agents, proves the result with numbers and gets paid for it. It works for a one-person shop serving a ten-person company, and it scales to a team inside a large enterprise.

From Vas Adapted

Source: Vas of Varick Agents, on Greg Isenberg's Startup Ideas Podcast, plus his talk slides and an interview about process mining. A green tag marks ideas and numbers taken from that material. A gold tag marks anything added for this playbook, such as small-business timelines and scripts.

What an FDE does

A forward deployed engineer works inside the customer's operation and owns an outcome: fewer days to close the books, fewer quotes that go quiet, a lower cost per invoice. A software vendor ships a product and leaves. An FDE ships a changed process and stays long enough to measure it.

The central claim of the playbook is that applied AI fails when it starts with a tool. The client describes a process with five steps. The actual process has twenty, with loops nobody drew. An agent built on the five-step version automates the wrong thing. So the work starts with the process and ends with the numbers.

Overview

The whole loop on one page

Eight stages, always in this order. Skipping the baseline is the most common way to lose the case study.

  1. MapFind the real process by interviewing people and reading the systems.
  2. MeasureSeparate waiting time from working time. Pick one owner.
  3. SortDelete, plain code, agent or human, for every step.
  4. BuildInside the tools the client already uses.
  5. ProveSame map and same metrics, measured again.
  6. SellPitch the outcome to the person who owns the number.
  7. PriceFree first, then fixed, then a slice of the value.
  8. RunA dated plan: audit, build, then re-audit.

Small business (1–50 people)

Knowledge lives in the owner's head, in WhatsApp, in email and in a spreadsheet. You interview, pull exports, build one workflow and re-measure at 30 days.

Enterprise

Knowledge lives in the CRM and ERP, in old documents and in long-tenured staff. You mine the systems for weeks, build across many instances of the same stack and audit at 3 and 6 months.

Stage 1

Map the real process

Ask what people actually do, then check it against what the systems recorded. Where the two disagree you have found the work.

Three sources, in this order From Vas

  1. People, the "human API". Start with the head of one department and work down to the people who do the work. The details that matter usually live with long-tenured staff, not in written procedures. Finance is a good first department: payables, receivables, reconciliations, billing, banking, planning.
  2. Systems of record. Get live access to the CRM or ERP (Salesforce, NetSuite, Dynamics, Workday) for three to four weeks and track inputs, changes and handoffs. This gives you an empirical graph of how records move.
  3. Documents. Collect everything, current or stale: SharePoint, Drive, Notion, Slack, Teams, Gmail, spreadsheets.

The six interview questions From Vas

  1. Why do you do it this way today?
  2. Who really decides?
  3. Which step is theater?
  4. Which step is legitimate?
  5. What happens when there is an exception?
  6. How often do exceptions happen?
Adapted · how to run the interview

Ask for the last three real cases, from first contact to paid or lost, and walk each one step by step. Stories give you the exceptions. Asking "what is your process?" gives you the brochure.

Small business

  • No ten-year system history exists, so lean on interviews.
  • Export the WhatsApp, email and spreadsheet history. Timestamps in chats tell you real reply times.
  • If there is no system of record, put them on one. That is part of the job.

Enterprise

  • Run process mining on the CRM or ERP for 3–4 weeks.
  • Interview from department head down to individual contributors.
  • Reconcile documented steps, mined steps and interview answers into one loop map.

Stage 2

Measure the time, pick one owner

Every process has two clocks. Touch time is the minutes someone actually works on it. Elapsed time is how long the item exists. In most processes the gap is the problem: work sits in an inbox, waits for approval or waits for a reply.

From Vas Count idle days as a metric of their own. Clients rarely have the number and are surprised by it.

One process, one owner

Pick one process with a named person who owns the result. That person approves the map, signs off on the baseline and accepts the outcome. A sponsor with budget sits above them. Without an owner the agent becomes an IT project and nobody feels it fail.

Adapted · small business

In a family business the owner of the process is usually the person who answers the customers, and the sponsor is the person who pays. Say both names out loud in the first meeting.

Stage 3

Sort every step into four buckets

With the real map in hand, take each step and ask four questions in order. The first yes decides the bucket.

  1. Does anyone still need this step to exist?Copying a contact into a second spreadsheet, an alert email nobody reads.
    No → Delete
  2. Is the rule always the same, with clean inputs?If x then y. Triggers, record creation, template replies, timers.
    Yes → Plain code
  3. Does it need judgment from messy input or history?Reading a vague message, categorizing, drafting a quote or a follow-up.
    Yes → Agent
  4. Is a mistake costly or hard to undo?Money leaving, contracts, legal promises, high-value quotes. Applies to the output of agent steps too.
    Yes → Human decides

From Vas The buckets. The rule he returns to: the agent prepares and a person decides. Agents do the work that needs judgment but not liability.

Adapted The example is a service business. In Vas's accounting-firm case, the 14 steps sorted into 1 delete, 3 agent, 2 human and the rest plain code. Most steps end up as plain code, which is why this is cheap to run.

Write the starting numbers down and get the owner to initial them. Without a baseline you cannot show a result, and the case study is the product that sells your next project.

Stage 4

Build inside the client's tools

Put the agent where the work already happens. A company that spent years and about $10 million migrating from one ERP to the next will not adopt a new system for your agent. A shop that lives in WhatsApp will not open a new dashboard every morning.

Rules for building From Vas

  • Work in the system of record. Salesforce, NetSuite, Workday, Dynamics at enterprise scale. A spreadsheet, a CRM table or a database for a small shop. If there is none, install one.
  • Send approvals where people already talk. Slack or Teams in a company, WhatsApp or SMS in a shop.
  • Prefer background agents to sidekicks. A chat copilot makes people 10 to 20 percent faster. An agent that runs a recurring workflow silently and pings a person only on exceptions delivered 70 to 80 percent faster output in his examples.
  • Benchmark models per step. Try several, including open-source and cheaper models. Use the smallest one that holds up.
  • Build once, deploy everywhere. Group clients by stack (all NetSuite together, all HubSpot together) so a connector built once serves many.

When to re-measure

Enterprise From Vas

Audit at 3 and 6 months after deployment against the original baseline and show the numbers to the executive sponsor.

Small business Adapted

A small shop will not wait six months. Re-measure at day 30 and again at day 90. Book both dates before you start building.

Case study template

  1. The business and the one process, in two sentences.
  2. Before: the map, the baseline table, the date it was measured.
  3. What changed: the four-bucket sort and what the agent does.
  4. After: the same map and table, the date, and what was left to humans.
  5. One quote from the process owner.
  6. What it costs to run each month.

Stage 6

Sell the outcome to the person who owns the number

Do not sell AI. Sell a changed number to the person who is judged on it. You sell twice: once with a map of their mess before you build, and again with the result afterwards.

BuyerThey care aboutOpening question
CFOCost, margin, accuracy, speed of month-end close"How long does it take you to close your books?" (22 days is typical in the talk, with 4 to 8 days as the target.)
CRO or sales leadQuotes sent, deals closed, cycle time"How many quotes go out and never get a follow-up?"
CHRO or people leadHiring faster, day-one onboarding, leverage for the team"How long until a new hire is productive?" Pitch leverage, not cuts.
CIO or CTOOutput, modernization, staying inside current systems"Which systems must this live inside?"
Small-business owner AdaptedLost jobs, evenings spent on messages, getting paid"When a quote goes out, how many days before you chase it?"

From Vas Frame the result as reallocating people. The agent removes administrative load and the strongest staff move to higher-leverage work such as planning or becoming internal FDEs. Other numbers from the talk: tax returns 30 percent faster at 98 percent accuracy, and in roll-ups, doubling gross margin on a $1B purchase can turn into a 2x to 8x resale value.

Getting a first client with no track record From Vas

  • Use warm contacts or cold outreach, and make Friday the day you do it.
  • Offer one simple workflow, end to end, on a fixed timeline.
  • Do the first project free. The experience and the measured result are worth more than the first fee, because they let you charge five or six figures on the next one.

He gives no word-for-word script. A short one is under Call scripts.

Common objections Adapted

"We tried AI and it did nothing."
It was probably aimed at the documented process. We map the real one first and measure before we build.
"Will this replace my staff?"
It removes the typing and chasing. The person still decides prices and promises, and spends the saved time on work that needs them.
"We don't have time."
You need two short interviews and one owner who approves the numbers. We do the rest.
"Can't we just buy a tool?"
You can, and it still needs mapping to your process. We start from the map, and we use tools you already have where we can.

Stage 7

Price it

Price against the value created, not against your hours. Early on you have no proof, so you start free and climb.

  1. FreeFirst project
    Buys the baseline, the case study and the experience. Trade it for data access, a quote and permission to publish the result. From Vas
  2. Fixed feeEarly projects
    A set build price plus a monthly fee that covers running cost and support. Easy for a small business to approve. Adapted
  3. Five figuresSecond project
    With one measured result in hand, projects move from free or low five figures to high five figures. From Vas
  4. Value sliceEnterprise
    About 10 percent of the value delivered. Value of $10M gives a $1M fee. Top FDEs reach $1M a year by delivering $5M to $25M in value. From Vas
  5. CarryPrivate equity
    A small ownership share, for example 0.5 percent, tied to the increase in portfolio value. From Vas

Price from the baseline Adapted

The talk does not cover monthly retainers, so use this arithmetic. Recovered revenue per month is the quotes nobody followed up, times the share that reply after a follow-up, times the share that close, times the average job value.

Calibrate to the market you are in Adapted

Enterprise anchors do not transfer to a small shop in a low-income market. Check what local alternatives cost (a part-time assistant, a local bot builder, a subscription tool) and price below the cost of doing nothing.

Call scripts

The 20-minute discovery call Adapted

Built on Vas's interview questions. Use it for a first call with a small-business owner or a department head.

MinutesGoalSay
0–5Find how work enters and who touches it"Walk me through what happened with your last three enquiries, from the first message to paid or lost. Where did each one arrive, and who read it first?"
5–12Find the theater and the exceptions"Why do you do it that way? Who decides? Which step is legitimate and which is just habit? What happens when something goes wrong, and how often?"
12–17Get baseline numbers"How long from the first message to a quote? After a quote goes out, how many days before anyone follows up? How many never get a follow-up?"
17–20Frame the outcome and agree dates"We build inside what you already use, and you stay the one who approves. Who owns this process, who is the sponsor, and when do we re-measure?"

A cold message for a first project Adapted

Hi [Name]. I help [trade] businesses stop losing quotes that go quiet. I'd like to map one workflow with you, from the first enquiry to the quote follow-up, and build a fix for it at no charge. In return I would ask for your numbers before and after, and permission to write it up. It takes two short conversations from you. Worth 20 minutes this week?

Pitfalls

  1. Starting with the tool. Map first, then choose.
  2. Trusting the documented process. The real one is longer and has loops.
  3. Skipping the baseline. No before numbers means no case study.
  4. Automating a step that should be deleted. Ask whether the step is needed before you build it.
  5. Giving an agent authority over money or contracts. Those stay with a person.
  6. Making the client log into something new. Build inside the tools they have.
  7. Taking several processes at once. One process, one owner.
  8. Promising benchmark numbers. Case-study figures show a pattern. The client's own baseline sets the target.
  9. Measuring once. Re-measure on the dates you agreed.

Glossary

Forward deployed engineer (FDE)
An engineer embedded in a customer's operation who owns a measured outcome.
System of record
The system that holds the official data: a CRM, an ERP, or for a small shop a spreadsheet or database.
Process mining
Reading system activity logs to reconstruct how work actually flows, including loops.
Human API
Using interviews to extract procedures that live in people's heads.
Touch time and elapsed time
Minutes of real work versus calendar time the item exists.
Idle days
Days an item sits with nobody working on it.
Straight-through processing
The share of items handled with no human touch.
Background agent
An agent that runs a recurring workflow on its own and alerts a person on exceptions.
Sidekick
A chat copilot a person talks to while working.
Human in the loop
A step where a person makes the final call.
Baseline
The measured starting values of the metrics, recorded before building.
Value slice
A fee set as a share of the value the project creates.
Carry
An ownership share in the increase of a company's value, used in private equity deals.
